What you are looking at

A timetable is a list of trains and the stations they serve. Turning it into a network forces a choice: what is an edge? The paper builds three answers, each as a directed, weighted graph:

  • Stations — consecutive stations on a train's path, including those it runs through without stopping. This is close to the physical network.
  • Stops — consecutive stopping points only. Express trains now create long edges that skip stations.
  • Changes — every pair of stations you can travel between without changing trains. Each train becomes a fully connected group.

Each space is weighted by the number of trains on an edge, or by the inverse of the mean travel time. Clustering the graphs (the paper uses Infomap) reveals which stations the timetable binds together — and the clusters differ between spaces. The paper uses this, with a Timetable Connectivity Index, to compare the Norwegian 2024 timetable with a planned 2033 one.

Simplifications on this page: the timetable is a made-up 12-station toy (below), not the Norwegian data. Edges are drawn undirected, and clusters come from a small modularity heuristic (greedy local moves) rather than Infomap. No code from the authors' GPL-3.0 repository is used.
