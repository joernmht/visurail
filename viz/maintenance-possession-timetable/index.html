Field guide

Planned works, planned timetable

Track renewals, platform rebuilds and new signalling need the track itself. For hours, days or weeks, part of the network is a possession: closed to trains and handed to the works. The regular timetable then no longer fits, and someone has to design a new one. In the Netherlands that was a team of about 40 planners, starting six months ahead. Producing one feasible timetable for one day of works took about 14 weeks, by hand, one small part of the network at a time.

Van Aken, Bešinović and Goverde turn this into an optimisation problem, the train timetable adjustment problem (TTAP). It takes the original periodic timetable and the list of closed tracks and platforms, and finds the alternative timetable that changes the least. It may use four measures: retiming (delays only, so no passenger misses a train that leaves early), reordering, short-turning at the last station before a closed section where a train can reverse, and cancelling. Delay is counted on arrivals, because that is what passengers notice. One cancelled line weighs as much as 100 000 seconds of delay, so the model only cancels when no delay within the limit works.

The model extends the periodic event scheduling problem (PESP), the standard model for cyclic timetables. Short-turning is decided before the optimisation, by a preprocessing step that splits train lines at the closure. Two further ideas make it work on real networks. A binary variable per event lets a delay cross the hour boundary, so a train delayed past :59 runs at :01 instead of being cancelled. And station capacity constraints, which count the trains present at a station, are added by row generation: only for stations where a solution actually overloads the platforms, and then the model is solved again. That kept the largest case, the Randstad area with about a third of all Dutch train events, solvable in under seven minutes.

Simplifications: one made-up five-station double-track corridor with eight train lines per hour, not the Dutch network. Each train piece is shifted as a whole (the paper retimes every event), times are in whole minutes, platform tracks stand for all station tracks, and the search is a small exact branch-and-bound in your browser instead of a MILP solved with CPLEX. Short-turning, the delay-only rule, the hour-crossing rule and the objective weights (100 000 per cancelled line, 1 per second of arrival delay) follow the paper. The figures for the Dutch case studies in the essay come from the paper's tables.
